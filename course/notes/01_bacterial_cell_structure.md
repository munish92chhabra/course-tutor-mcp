# Bacterial Cell Structure

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture a student should be able to describe the major
structural components of a bacterial cell, explain how the Gram-positive and
Gram-negative envelopes differ, and relate each structure to a function the
cell depends on.

## The cell envelope

Every bacterial cell is bounded by a plasma membrane, and almost all are
surrounded by a cell wall outside it. The wall is what gives the cell its
shape and what stops it bursting: a growing bacterium in a dilute medium has
an internal osmotic pressure of several atmospheres, and the wall is the only
thing holding against it. Remove the wall enzymatically and the cell rounds
up into a protoplast, which survives only in an osmotically balanced medium.

The wall polymer is peptidoglycan, also called murein. It is a mesh of glycan
chains, alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid, cross-linked
to each other by short peptide bridges. The cross-linking is what makes it a
single covalently continuous molecule around the whole cell, effectively one
enormous bag-shaped macromolecule.

## Gram-positive and Gram-negative envelopes

The Gram stain divides most bacteria into two groups, and the division
reflects a real structural difference rather than a staining artefact.

Gram-positive cells have a thick peptidoglycan layer, perhaps twenty to forty
layers deep, sitting directly outside the plasma membrane. Threaded through it
are teichoic acids, anionic polymers that run through the wall and, in the
case of lipoteichoic acid, anchor into the membrane itself.

Gram-negative cells have a thin peptidoglycan layer, often only one or two
layers, and outside it a second membrane. This outer membrane is asymmetric:
the inner leaflet is ordinary phospholipid, but the outer leaflet is
lipopolysaccharide, or LPS. The space between the two membranes, containing
the thin wall, is the periplasm.

The practical consequence is permeability. The Gram-negative outer membrane is
a barrier that excludes many antibiotics and detergents, which is a large part
of why Gram-negative infections are harder to treat. Small hydrophilic
molecules cross it through porins, channel proteins that act as a size filter.

## Why the Gram stain works

Crystal violet enters both cell types and is fixed in place by iodine as a
large crystal violet-iodine complex. The decolourising step, usually ethanol
or acetone, is the discriminating one. In Gram-positive cells the thick,
heavily cross-linked wall dehydrates and its pores close, trapping the complex
inside. In Gram-negative cells the alcohol dissolves the lipid-rich outer
membrane and the complex washes out through the thin wall. The safranin
counterstain then colours the now-colourless Gram-negative cells pink.

Two common sources of error follow directly from this. Over-decolourising
makes Gram-positive cells read as Gram-negative, and old cultures give
unreliable results because dying Gram-positive cells lose wall integrity.
This is why the stain should be done on a young culture, ideally in
exponential phase.

## Structures outside the wall

A capsule is a well-organised layer of polysaccharide attached outside the
wall; a slime layer is the same kind of material, less firmly attached. Both
resist phagocytosis, and capsule loss is a classic way that a virulent strain
becomes avirulent.

Flagella drive motility. The filament is a helical polymer of flagellin,
turned like a propeller by a rotary motor embedded in the envelope and driven
by the proton motive force rather than by ATP directly. Fimbriae and pili are
shorter and thinner; fimbriae mediate attachment to surfaces, while the sex
pilus mediates contact during conjugation.

## The cytoplasm

The bacterial chromosome is typically a single circular molecule of DNA,
supercoiled into a region called the nucleoid, which is not membrane-bound.
Plasmids are smaller circular DNA molecules that replicate independently and
often carry accessory genes, including antibiotic resistance determinants.

Ribosomes are 70S, made of 50S and 30S subunits. The difference from the 80S
eukaryotic ribosome is the basis of selective toxicity for several antibiotic
classes, which is worth remembering when you meet them later in the course.

Some species form endospores: dormant, dehydrated, heat-resistant structures
produced inside the mother cell in response to starvation. Their resistance is
the reason sterilisation protocols are written the way they are, which we come
back to in the sterilisation lecture.
